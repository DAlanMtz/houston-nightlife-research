Chapter 03 · Growth and concentration

More venues,
a smaller venue.

The sector added venues for a decade. After 2022 it stopped getting larger in real terms, and the typical venue became smaller.

Houston's strict-city nightlife population grew from 576 annual ever-active venues in 2013 to 805 in 2025. Combined real reported receipts rose 17%, from $442.9 million to $519.1 million. The count rose faster, 40%, so receipts per venue fell on average by 16%, from $768,930 to $644,788. The median fell further, 26%, from $486,931 to $361,409.

The sector did not shrink. It became broader and thinner: more establishments, each reporting less at the typical level. The pattern holds through the 2020 collapse and the partial recovery that followed, and it shows up in the median rather than the total.1

The count series and the receipt series diverge in 2020. Annual ever-active venues barely moved, from 690 in 2019 to 684 in 2020, because most venues reported at least one positive month before the shutdown. December-active stock fell from 624 to 549. Real receipts fell 49%, from $541.6 million to $275.9 million. A single annual count would have hidden both the timing and the depth of the break.2

Real receipts grew 22% from 2013 to 2019, fell by half in 2020, recovered to $535.3 million in 2021, and peaked at $614.9 million in 2022. They then fell for three consecutive years: $590.9 million in 2023, $542.3 million in 2024, and $519.1 million in 2025. The 2025 total is 4% below 2019, 16% below the 2022 peak, and 17% above 2013.

The per-venue series is more informative. Median real receipts were roughly flat from 2013 to 2019 and peaked in 2015 at $534,552. After the 2020 collapse they recovered to $461,239 by 2022 but never returned to the 2013–2019 range. The 2025 median is 26% below 2013 and 22% below 2022. The mean fell 16% over the full period, from $768,930 to $644,788.

An identity, not a decomposition

Total receipts equal active venues multiplied by mean receipts per venue. Between 2013 and 2025 the first factor rose 40% and the second fell 16%, so the product rose 17%.

That arithmetic does not show that entry caused the growth in total receipts, or that competition caused the decline in per-venue receipts. Demand, income, rents, formats, and composition could all move both terms.

3.3

Receipts became modestly more concentrated.

The venue-level Gini in the primary band rose from 0.5642 to 0.5953, a change of +0.0311. The top decile's share rose from 39.4% to 41.6%. No single venue dominates: the largest held 1.4% of 2025 receipts and the five largest together held 5.9%.

The direction is stable across classification bands; the magnitude is not. The lower band rises from 0.485 to 0.598 and the upper band from 0.605 to 0.642. The in/uncertain boundary therefore changes how large the concentration result looks, even though it does not change its direction.3

The concentration finding is descriptive. It does not establish operator concentration, market power, or pricing behavior. A rising venue-level Gini is consistent with a few large venues growing faster, with small venues entering, or with both.

Chapter 04 · Changing geography

The centre moved
east-northeast.

Activity became more clustered even as the city added venues. A fifth of those venues still sits outside any named district.

The real-receipt-weighted centre of nightlife activity moved about 1.6 km east-northeast between 2013 and 2025. The unweighted venue centre moved about 1.0 km in a compatible direction. Dispersion around the weighted centre fell from 16.0 km to 14.5 km, so activity became slightly more spatially concentrated even as the number of venues grew.

These are movements in where taxable receipts are reported. They are not evidence that venues, customers, or investment moved in the same way. A district can gain receipts because venues opened, because survivors grew, because reporting moved across a boundary, or because one large venue opened.1

4.1

Three geographies, kept separate.

The analysis uses 73 fixed named districts, a dynamic cluster layer built from a frozen DBSCAN specification, and a continuous spatial summary that needs neither boundary. Districts answer named comparisons. Clusters answer concentration. The continuous layer answers movement. Each answers a different question.

4.2

Receipts moved toward the centre and the Heights.

Among the 21 of 73 named districts with no suppressed year, the only ones compared here, real receipts grew most in Greater Heights (+$32.5M, +384%), Downtown (+$24.1M, +69.8%) and Greenway/Upper Kirby (+$11.3M, +64.7%), followed by Neartown–Montrose and Washington Avenue. They fell most in Mid West (−$12.2M, −43.3%) and Greater Uptown (−$8.6M, −30.4%). This ranks a subset, not all districts: the other 52 have at least one suppressed year, and some could rank among these. Published district values cover about 71–80% of city receipts depending on the year, so citywide totals are not the sum of the visible district values. These are movements in where taxable receipts are reported, not evidence of neighborhood improvement, decline, displacement, or policy effects. A district can gain receipts because venues opened, because survivors grew, because reporting moved across a boundary, or because a single large venue opened.

4.3

The clusters captured more of the activity.

The share of city real receipts inside the frozen dynamic clusters rose from 0.278 in 2013 to 0.481 in 2023, then eased to 0.404 in 2025. The share of venues in clusters, derived for this report from the continuous spatial summary layer, rose from 0.219 to 0.321 over the same period.2 Because the frozen specification fixes cluster membership, the change measures where activity sits inside a fixed geography. It cannot show new clusters emerging.

District-level concentration points the same way with a different unit. Normalized entropy fell from 0.785 to 0.747, and the receipt HHI rose from 0.055 to 0.064. These are computed on all geocoded receipts, including districts that are suppressed in the public edition.

4.4

What the geography cannot establish.

Coverage is the larger limitation. Between 77% and 81% of strict-city venues assign to a named district, 12–16% are unassigned, and a further 6–7% lack a usable geocode. That leaves 19–23% of venues in pseudo-district rows. A district trajectory could shift if those venues were resolved. The district polygons are current-vintage, not historical.3

Cluster lineage depends on overlap thresholds: a missed continuation link fragments a lineage and inflates the apparent "emerging" share. The decomposition that assigns growth to continuing, entering, and exiting venues is descriptive, and its lineage terms remain on hold.

Chapter 06 · Closure and competing risks

Disappearance
is not closure.

Most exits are replacements at the same address. About one in six venues cannot be classified at all, and the record does not observe a transition.

Of the 8,445 panel venues, 4,324 were operating at the panel end, 2,499 are closed, 180 relocated, 113 dormant, 5 likely closed, and 1,324 unknown. The unknown state is 15.7% of the panel. These are final-state classifications, not observed transitions.

A tax filing stops when a venue stops filing. It does not say whether the business closed, changed ownership, changed concept, moved, or simply went quiet. Phase 7 classifies each venue from reporting continuity, TABC responsibility and status evidence, documented successors, relocation edges, and post-panel records. The evidence mix matters because a venue can carry several types.1

6.1

Six final states.

Operating is the largest state, followed by closed. Unknown is larger than relocated, dormant, and likely closed combined. That ordering is the main caution here: the second-largest category of disappearance is not a confirmed exit at all.

The 8,445 figure covers a different population from the 1,667 headline group. It includes every classification band and every geography. A closure rate computed on 8,445 is not a rate for the 1,667, and it should not be read as one.

6.2

Closed is not one thing.

Within the 2,499 closed states, 1,074 are ordinary closures and 1,425 are concept-successor replacements at the same premise. Replacement, not vacancy, is the more common exit. The cumulative incidence is 0.169 for concept replacement and 0.126 for ordinary closure.2

A further 5 venues are likely closed and censored in the primary competing-risk definition. That is why the classified exit-type count of ordinary closures is 1,079 while the competing-risk event count is 1,064.

6.3

Events, censoring, and timing.

2,668 venues have a validated exit event, 5,777 are censored at the panel end, and 5,777 + 2,668 = 8,445. Cumulative incidence over the full panel is 0.169 for concept replacement, 0.126 for ordinary closure, and 0.021 for relocation. These are descriptive proportions of the panel, not annual hazards.

Event timing is uneven in precision: 336 events are exact or near-exact, 1,118 are month-level, and 1,214 are bounded intervals. The interval width has a median of 1 month, but the 75th percentile is 10 months, the 90th is 29, and the maximum is 153. A closure "date" is therefore often a window.3

6.4

What remains unknown.

1,324 venues (15.7%) have insufficient evidence for any state except unknown. This is the largest single limitation on closure analysis. The project leaves them censored: it does not impute them, treat them as survivors, or drop them from the panel.

A further 104 venues have known post-panel events, 92 of them administrative status cessations. In documented cases, venues that were analytically operating at the June 2026 panel end closed later. Those events are preserved outside the primary risk period and are never backdated.4

Read this before quoting a closure rate

Final-state labels are classifications, not transitions. The record does not observe a venue moving from operating to closed, and this report does not present a state-to-state flow as if it did.
